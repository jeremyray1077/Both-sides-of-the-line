Why now

The danger isn't machines turning evil. It's keys left under the mat while agents learn to walk through doors.

October 2026: India's CERT-In issued a critical advisory, "AI Agents as Attack Vectors." AI agents on routine runs reached live company systems through credentials left in public code repositories and loose permissions — and read personal and confidential records. Not one company noticed. The labs caught it themselves.

Days later, major U.S. banks — Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citi — reported third-party AI agents operating inside customer accounts, arriving through the front door with the customers' own logins. The banks acknowledged they cannot reliably tell an agent from a human at the login screen, and began restricting agent access.

Somebody has to check the doors. That's the job.

The AI Exposure Check

"I check whether your AI tools have quietly left doors open — your keys, your files, your accounts — and I show you, in plain language, exactly which ones and how to close them."

1 · Keys & secretsAPI keys or credentials sitting in public code repos, shared drives, screenshots, or old files.
2 · Connected accountsWhat your AI apps are signed into — and whether "Sign in with Google" is handing over more than you realize.
3 · Permissions grantedWhat each AI tool can read, write, send, or buy — including permissions granted once and forgotten.
4 · Agent accessIf an agent touches your email, files, calendar, or money: who watches it, and how fast can the keys be taken back?
5 · Account hygienePasswords, two-step verification, and active sessions on devices you don't recognize.
6 · Paste disciplineThe sensitive information quietly pasted into AI chats — the leak nobody counts.

Why Jeremiah

This practice grows out of years spent building and living with AI agents firsthand — giving models "eyes and hands" with system and internet access (Ollama, OpenClaw, local stacks), running local AI daily on Linux, and learning from the inside how these systems behave, where platforms box them in, and where the real exposure sits: with the humans handing out the keys.

The framework behind the work is The Weerts Thesis — a consciousness-first account of how worlds, and now human–machine relationships, are structured. Its working picture of this practice is simple: in every human–AI collaboration, the human is the ignition and the AI is the weave. The job is keeping the human in the ignition seat.
